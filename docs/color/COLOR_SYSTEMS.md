## Recommended: **System A — warm near-white with a deep ink accent**

**Why it wins:**

1. **It is warm enough to leave the category and cool enough to avoid the trend.** `#FBFAF7` is a near-white with a trace of warmth — it reads as paper, not as beige. System B's `#F7F4EE` is where "warm" starts becoming a colour, and it scores 4 on beige-trend risk accordingly.
2. **Best long-form readability.** Slightly reduced contrast against pure white, without losing headroom.
3. **Maximum distance from the measured category** — every cybersecurity site inspected sits on black or near-black.
4. **Accessibility headroom.** A near-white ground makes AAA achievable for body text and comfortable AA for the apparatus, which matters because subordination must come from size and register rather than from low contrast.

**Rejected:**

- **B** — the most likeable and the most dangerous. `#F7F4EE` plus terracotta is precisely the warm-editorial trend palette; it would read as a design portfolio, which is the F3 failure.
- **C** — competent and anonymous. Cool neutral plus slate blue is the default contemporary product palette; it concedes the differentiation entirely (5/10 distance from category).
- **D** — pure white, pure black, no accent. Genuinely austere and the highest accessibility headroom, but it reads as an academic PDF (academic risk 3) and leaves links with no affordance beyond underline. **Retained as the fallback if warmth proves unworkable in dark mode.**

---

## The single most important value

**The ground.** Everything else follows from it.

`#FBFAF7` — a ~2% warm shift off white. The test: **placed beside pure white it should be perceptibly warmer; viewed alone it should read as white paper, not as a coloured background.**

If 5D shows it reading as cream or beige, the correction is toward white (`#FCFBF9`), not toward more warmth.

# Anti-Brand

**Date:** 2026-08-14 (Phase 2)

What Het's brand is **not**. Each entry states why it is dangerous *for him specifically* and how it is avoided structurally rather than by good intentions.

---

## 1. NOT a generic cybersecurity portfolio

**Why dangerous:** Phase 1 measured the archetype. Independent sites by independent authors share section order (About → Skills → Services → Projects → Contact), percentage skill bars, tools-as-portfolio, and filler vocabulary. **Het's toolset is identical to the archetype's** — Burp, Nmap, Wireshark, Nikto. Presented as inventory, he is indistinguishable.

**How avoided:** the unit is an investigation, not a skills list. Tools appear inside a described method, never as a section. No skills bars, no services, no fixed section order.

---

## 2. NOT a hacker persona

**Why dangerous:** it repels the audience Het's scarcest asset serves. GRC, audit and regulated-finance readers are risk-averse by profession. The raifkaya.nl heading *"Master skills that are beyond legal"* is the extreme case, but any cyberpunk styling costs credibility with the exact people who value his CSCRF/IRDAI exposure.

**Also:** Phase 1's revision found this is no longer even differentiating — the stronger peer portfolios have already abandoned it. It is now simply a cost with no benefit.

**How avoided:** banned outright — neon green, matrix, glitch, monospace-as-body, terminal chrome, status theatre. The measured values are recorded in `../research/REJECTED_PATTERNS.md`.

---

## 3. NOT a consultancy

**Why dangerous:** Het seeks employment, not engagements. A "Services" section appeared on **all three** inspected student portfolios — inherited template furniture that signals confusion about purpose. Worse, a consultancy frame invites comparison to actual consultancies, which he loses on every axis.

**How avoided:** no services, no pricing, no "work with me", no client language. He had no clients of his own; he was an intern on a team.

---

## 4. NOT a resume website

**Why dangerous:** if the site's content is the resume rearranged, it has no reason to exist. The resume already proves *exposure*; a site that only repeats it adds nothing.

**How avoided:** the site answers what the resume structurally cannot — how he reasons. Credentials appear as compact metadata, never as headline sections. **No "COURSES COMPLETED" or "LICENSES ACQUIRED" counting.**

---

## 5. NOT a personal blog

**Why dangerous:** brief §35 forbids it, and the failure mode is specific — an open-ended blog with three posts and no fourth reads as abandonment. Het's writing capacity is unverified, so a format that *requires* cadence is a bet against unknown odds.

**How avoided:** investigations are retrospective, written from work already done, not scheduled. Field notes are short, numbered and finishable (the Setosa model). Neither format promises a cadence.

---

## 6. NOT a certification wall

**Why dangerous:** Het holds 16 Credly certifications plus several programme certificates. Displayed prominently, accumulation reads as early-career anxiety, and certificate count is precisely what a technical reader discounts. adharvkt.github.io demonstrates the failure — "COURSES COMPLETED", "INTERNSHIPS ATTENDED", "LICENSES ACCQUIRED" as counted sections.

**How avoided:** certifications listed once, factually, low in the hierarchy. Never counted, never a headline, never a badge wall. CompTIA Security+ shown as in progress without ceremony.

---

## 7. NOT a project dump

**Why dangerous:** two ways to fail. Today: **there is nothing to dump** — zero verified original repositories. Later: repository count becomes the metric, which invites comparison to peers with more. The Phase 1 peer had eight deployed projects; that contest is unwinnable now and unnecessary later.

**How avoided:** artifacts are outputs *attached to* investigations, not entries in their own right. Volume is never surfaced as a number. No "8 projects" counter.

---

## 8. NOT a fake security operations centre

**Why dangerous:** simulated dashboards, fake alert feeds, invented threat counters and mock terminals report nothing real. A status indicator that does not reflect a measured condition is a lie rendered in UI — and to the practitioners Het needs to impress, instantly recognisable as one.

**How avoided:** no simulated state. If a live element exists, it must display genuinely measured data (e.g. a real last-commit timestamp). Otherwise it does not exist.

---

## 9. NOT an AI-generated "tech" aesthetic

**Why dangerous:** this is the most current risk and the hardest to see from inside. Its markers: uniformly confident copy, tidy resolved narratives, symmetrical three-card layouts, gradient-on-dark, "Here's a thing nobody tells you" constructions, and the absence of any admitted uncertainty. Phase 1 found a peer site characterised as achieving *"intentional genericization through absence of personality markers"* — competent, credible, forgettable.

**How avoided — structurally, not stylistically:**
- **Required uncertainty.** Generated copy is uniformly confident; a site that systematically states what it could not determine is structurally the opposite.
- **First-person reasoning** including false starts and dead ends.
- **An "Unresolved" status** that is genuinely used.
- **Varied shape** — if every piece follows setup → discovery → limitation, the template becomes visible and the advantage is lost.

---

## 10. NOT a claim of seniority

**Why dangerous:** Het's roadmap targets "Senior, Staff, Principal and Distinguished" roles. His verified position is a recent graduate with a ~7-month internship. Any drift toward the roadmap's register would be immediately falsifiable — and overclaiming is, per both the research and his own strategy document, the fastest way to lose a technically strong reader.

**How avoided:** no senior/expert/architect/authority vocabulary. Role precision required ("I worked on", not "I led"). The roadmap is always labelled adopted direction, never authored framework or achievement.

---

## The single test

If a proposed element cannot survive this question, it does not ship:

> **Would a security engineer who has read four hundred of these portfolios find this element informative, or would they recognise it and move on?**

Recognition is the failure condition.

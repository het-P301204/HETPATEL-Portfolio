## Where the scores turn

**Zero-content resilience is the column that decides it.** C, E and F all score 2–3 because each needs volume to mean anything — weighting needs items to weigh, grouping needs groups, and a dual register needs both registers occupied. Today there are three investigations and no published professional one. B scores 10 because a uniform record of three rows is a complete short record and looks like one.

**A's 4 on hiring clarity** is the finding that removes it. A recruiter scanning a column of interrogatives cannot answer "what has he worked on" — they get the shape of his thinking without the subject matter. The idea is excellent and lands one line lower down, as the framing sentence.

**D's 6 on genericity resistance and portfolio clarity** is not a criticism of 5B. It reflects one field — `2026-03` — which the 5F research identifies as the strongest archive signal after excerpts, and which 5B had no way of knowing when it was written.

**E's 10s are real and E still loses.** It is the best index in this table for a body of work that exists. It is unbuildable at three items.

**A's 4 on professional-work compatibility** is worth recording: question-led rows make the audit work — necessarily the most abstract, because the specifics are confidential — look like Het's most theoretical rather than his most valuable.

---

## The decision

**B — WORKING RECORD**, absorbing what survives from D and E.

The realistic contest was **B vs E**, and it is a sequencing question rather than a quality one. E is where the index should end up; B is what can launch. The correct move is to build B **so that E is a data change, not a redesign** — which is exactly what Kettle's structure permits, since his thematic groups sit above rows that are themselves uniform records.

D is not discarded. Its three-line row, its written framing line and its density discipline are the substance of B; what changes is one metadata field and the width.

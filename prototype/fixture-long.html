Why my detection kept firing four hours late

Status
closed
Origin
self-initiated
Started
2026-04-02
Published
2026-04-19

I wrote a small brute-force detection against authentication logs in a lab I had built, and it worked — but every alert arrived roughly four hours after the activity it described. Not occasionally. Every time, and by almost exactly the same amount. A detection that is consistently wrong by a fixed offset is more interesting than one that is erratic, because a constant means something is being converted rather than something being dropped.

The obvious explanation was that my pipeline was slow, so I measured that first and got it out of the way. From the moment a log line was written to the moment the rule evaluated it, the pipeline took under two seconds at every stage. There was no backlog, no retry queue, nothing waiting. Whatever the four hours were, they were not elapsed time.

That left the timestamps themselves. If the delay was not in the movement of the data, it had to be in what the data claimed about itself. So I stopped looking at the system and started looking at a single event as it appeared at three different points: as written by the source, as stored after ingestion, and as read by the rule.

Picking one event and following it end to end is slower than querying for a pattern, and I resisted it for about an hour before doing it. It was the right call and I should have made it sooner. Aggregate queries tell you that something is wrong across a population. A single traced event tells you where.

Worth writing down: I have now twice reached for a broad query when a single traced example would have been faster. It feels more thorough. It usually is not.

At the source, the event carried a local timestamp with no zone information — just a date and a time. After ingestion it carried a UTC timestamp. The two differed by the offset of the zone the source host was configured for. Nothing had been lost, and nothing had been delayed. The value had been reinterpreted.

The ingestion step was treating a zone-less timestamp as though it were already UTC, which is a defensible default and also the wrong one for this source. The source was writing local time. So an event that happened at 09:00 local was being stored as 09:00 UTC, which is a different instant, and every downstream comparison inherited that error.

Why it looked like lateness rather than wrongness

This is the part I found genuinely interesting, because the symptom pointed away from the cause. My rule used a sliding window over recent events. When the stored timestamps were shifted into the future relative to true time, events did not enter the window when they occurred — they entered it when the wall clock finally caught up to the value that had been written down. The detection was not running late. It was running exactly on time against timestamps that described a moment that had not happened yet.

That distinction matters for the fix. A late pipeline is a capacity problem and you solve it with more throughput. A mislabelled instant is a semantics problem and throughput makes it worse, because you process the wrong values faster. Had I trusted the symptom, I would have spent the afternoon tuning something that was already fast enough.

It also explains something I had noticed and set aside. Alerts for activity late in the evening sometimes never fired at all. Shifted forward, those events landed beyond the end of the retention window my lab was configured for, and were aged out before the wall clock reached them. I had assumed I was miscounting. I was not — those detections were genuinely being lost, and by the same mechanism.

Correcting the ingestion step to attach the source's actual zone before conversion removed the offset, and the detection began firing within seconds of the activity. I re-ran the same synthetic authentication traffic I had used to build the rule and compared alert times against the times the traffic generator recorded. They agreed to within the pipeline latency I had measured at the start.

What the fix did not cover

Attaching a zone at ingestion works because I know what zone this source is in. I configured the host. In anything larger than a lab that assumption fails in at least two ways I can describe and probably more that I cannot.

The first is that a host's zone can change — a rebuild, a migration, a policy applied across a fleet — and nothing in the log line announces it. The pipeline would keep applying the old offset and the error would return silently, without a symptom as legible as a constant four hours. It would look like a small number of events with implausible ordering, which is much harder to notice.

The second is daylight saving. My lab sits in a zone that does not observe it, so I never saw the problem. A source in a zone that does would produce an hour that occurs twice and an hour that never occurs at all, and a fixed offset attached at ingestion cannot represent either. The correct handling is for the source to emit an unambiguous instant in the first place, which is not something the collector can fix downstream.

I looked at whether I could detect the mismatch automatically rather than configure it — comparing the timestamp in the event against the time of arrival, and flagging sources whose difference is stable and non-zero. It works on the traffic I generated. I am not confident it generalises, because a source that is genuinely batching its output would produce the same stable difference for an entirely legitimate reason, and I could not think of a way to distinguish the two from the log line alone.

Checking that the fix was actually the fix

Having found an explanation that accounted for the symptom, I was aware that I had found an explanation rather than necessarily the explanation. The offset matched the zone difference, which is strong, but a matching number is not the same as a demonstrated mechanism. Two things could have been true at once: the timestamps could have been misread and something else could also have been delaying evaluation.

So I tried to break it deliberately. I set the source host to a different zone, one with an offset I had not seen before, and re-ran the same traffic without changing anything else. If my explanation was right, the lateness should change to match the new offset exactly. It did. I then set the source to emit UTC explicitly, which should remove the lateness entirely, and it did that too.

Two confirmations from opposite directions is the standard I have started holding myself to when the evidence is a coincidence of numbers. Predicting a new value and getting it is worth considerably more than explaining a value I had already seen, because the second is available to any explanation that happens to fit.

The one thing I could not construct was a negative case that behaved differently for a different reason. I wanted to introduce a genuine processing delay and confirm that it looked distinguishable from the timestamp problem, so that I would recognise the difference next time. I could add a delay, but everything I tried produced a variable lag rather than a constant one, which is exactly the distinction I already had. Useful, but it confirmed what I believed rather than testing it.

What I would keep from this

The most transferable part is not about timestamps. It is that the units in which a symptom is described tend to suggest the category of the cause, and that suggestion is not evidence. A problem described in hours invites a search for something slow. A problem described in counts invites a search for something dropping. Neither description constrains the cause at all, and I spent an hour inside the first of those before checking whether the magnitude was even plausible.

The second is about defaults. The collector did nothing wrong by its own lights, and the source did nothing wrong by its own lights, and the failure lived entirely in the space between two reasonable assumptions that nobody had written down. That space does not show up in either component's documentation because neither component owns it. I do not have a general method for finding those gaps, and I would like one.

What I could not determine is whether the ingestion default was a deliberate choice or an unexamined one. Assuming UTC for a zone-less timestamp is a reasonable policy: it is at least consistent, it is what several well-known collectors do, and the alternative — guessing the zone from the host — is wrong in a different and quieter way. If that was the reasoning, then the defect is not in the default but in my having pointed a source at it that does not satisfy the default's assumption.

I could not settle this because the environment was one I had assembled myself from stock configuration, so there was no change history to read and nobody to ask. The two possibilities call for different responses. If the default is deliberate, the correct fix is at the source — make it emit a zone — and my ingestion-side patch is a workaround that will mislead whoever finds it later. If it is an artefact, the collector should refuse a zone-less timestamp rather than silently assign one, because a rejected event is a visible failure and a reinterpreted event is an invisible one.

I have applied the ingestion-side fix and left a comment saying it is a workaround, which is the least satisfying of the available outcomes and the honest one. I would want to see how the collector behaves with a source that emits a zone correctly before arguing that its default is wrong, and I have not built that yet.

One further thing I want to record because it changed how I would approach this again. I spent the first hour on throughput because the symptom was described in units of time, and units of time suggest performance. The symptom's units were misleading. A four-hour constant was never plausible as latency in a pipeline I could observe end to end in two seconds — the magnitude alone should have ruled it out immediately, and I did not stop to check whether the explanation I was pursuing was even the right order of magnitude before pursuing it.

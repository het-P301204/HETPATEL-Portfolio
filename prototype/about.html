About

I am an early-career security practitioner. I test web applications, and I work on the compliance side of the same problem — which turns out to be the part most people skip.

What I work on

Two things, and the interesting part is where they meet.

The first is testing. Enumeration, then working through an application by hand in Burp Suite — injection, cross-site scripting, misconfiguration. Scanners are useful for the parts that should be automated and have never once told me the interesting thing. What I have found is that the difficult skill is not finding a weakness. It is writing it down so that the person who has to fix it understands what is actually wrong, and why it matters more than the four other things on their list.

The second is assurance — control mapping, audit documentation, the work of showing that something is managed rather than merely fixed. I came to this expecting it to be paperwork. It is not. Most of it reduces to a single question asked repeatedly: does this piece of evidence actually demonstrate the control it is filed under, or does it only appear to? That question is harder than it sounds and almost nobody writes about it.

Between the two sits the thing I find genuinely interesting: a finding and a control are not the same object. A tester asks whether something is exploitable. An audit asks whether the organisation can demonstrate that it manages the risk. Those are different questions, satisfied by different evidence, and the translation between them is where I have learned the most.

How I write this up

The investigations on this site are questions I worked through, written with what I found and what I could not determine. The last part is not modesty. An investigation that ends “and I could not establish which of these three explanations was responsible” is more useful to a reader than one that quietly picks the likeliest and moves on, because it tells them where the evidence actually stopped.

When I get something wrong I say so on the page, with a date, rather than editing it away. Where a conclusion has changed, the page says what it used to be and why it changed.

Experience

Info eShield Cyber Solutions — Cybersecurity Intern, VAPT, Risk & Compliance. January 2026 to present.

Web application penetration testing; identifying injection, cross-site scripting and misconfiguration, and documenting remediation. On the compliance side: risk assessment, control mapping against ISO 27001, and the documentation that supports an audit.

Through this role I have participated in CSCRF and IRDAI audit engagements in the financial sector. I assisted on that work — I did not scope it, own it or lead it, and I am not an auditor. What it gave me is the thing a lab cannot: seeing how a technical finding becomes a regulatory obligation, and how an obligation becomes evidence that somebody else has to accept.

Seven months is exposure, not expertise. My ISO 27001 knowledge is a working understanding of controls, risk management and compliance mapping, not a specialism. My Splunk experience is TryHackMe lab work, not production.

Where this is going

Toward security engineering — building the tooling and detection work I currently only read about, and continuing to write up the assurance side, which is under-documented relative to how much of the job it is.
